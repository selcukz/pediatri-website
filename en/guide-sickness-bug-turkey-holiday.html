A sickness bug on holiday in Turkey

It is the classic holiday disaster: day three, and a child who was fine at breakfast is being sick in a hotel bathroom a very long way from home. Most of these bugs are ordinary and settle in a few days on fluids alone. What makes them frightening is the setting — an unfamiliar country, a hotel doctor you have never met, and a flight home on Saturday. Here is how to think about all three.

What to do right now

If you are reading this at 2 a.m. in a hotel room, this is the whole of it:

  • Fluids, small and frequent. A spoonful or a few sips every few minutes beats a glass they immediately bring back up. This is the treatment; there usually isn't another one.
  • Oral rehydration solution is the one thing worth buying. Any Turkish pharmacy has it, and the diarrhoea and vomiting guide has the phrase to say at the counter and how to make it up safely here.
  • Stop the sun and the pool for the day. Heat and dehydration compound each other, and a child who is being sick should not be swimming anyway.
  • Do not force food. Appetite comes back on its own. Do not give anti-diarrhoeal medicines to a child without medical advice.
  • Watch the wet nappies, not the vomiting. What sends a child to hospital is dehydration, not how many times they have been sick.
This page is about the holiday — why it happens, what to avoid, who to see, and getting home. Why an all-inclusive holiday raises the risk

This is not folklore, and it is not really about Turkey. UK health authorities recorded a genuine rise in stomach infections among travellers returning from Turkey between July and October 2023, with a large share of cases among people who had stayed in all-inclusive resorts. The same pattern shows up in resort destinations everywhere, for four reasons that stack on top of each other.

Food held warm for hours

A buffet is, by design, cooked food kept at serving temperature for a long time. The gap between properly cold and properly hot is exactly the range in which bacteria multiply — so the risk is far more about temperature and time than about the cuisine.

Heat

Turkish summers on the coast run at 35–40 °C. Food spoils faster, children drink and swim more, and mild dehydration from the heat makes any tummy bug hit harder than the same bug would at home.

Crowds sharing everything

Hundreds of people, one pool, one splash pool, one set of serving tongs. Norovirus — the usual cause of sudden resort outbreaks of vomiting — needs only a tiny dose to infect, which is why it moves through a hotel in days.

A gut meeting new bacteria

Your child is encountering an ordinary local population of bacteria their immune system has never seen, in a week when they are eating unfamiliar food, staying up late and swimming all day. Adults get this too — children just show it faster.

The buffet, honestly

The useful advice here is narrower than the usual "be careful what you eat", which helps nobody on day one of a week's holiday. What matters is temperature, time and who handled it last.

SaferRiskier
Food that is piping hot, being cooked or replenished in front of youAnything lukewarm, or sitting in a tray you cannot tell the age of
The live cooking station — eggs, grills, pasta made to orderCold meats, and mayonnaise-based salads left out in the heat
Fruit you peel yourselfPre-cut fruit platters and cream-based desserts that have been out a while
Sealed bottled waterIce — often made from tap water even where the drinking water is bottled
Serving small children yourselfLetting a toddler work the shared tongs and then their own mouth

None of this means eating nervously for a week. Two habits carry most of the benefit: take food that is visibly hot, and wash the children's hands before they sit down — every time, including the beach lunch when it is least convenient.

Pool, splash pool and sea

The pool deserves more attention than it usually gets, because chlorine is not the guarantee people assume it is.

  • Out of the water for 48 hours after the last episode of diarrhoea or vomiting. This is the rule that actually stops outbreaks, and it is the one most often broken because the child feels better and the holiday is short.
  • Chlorine does not deal with everything quickly. Cryptosporidium, a common cause of holiday diarrhoea, survives normal pool chlorination for a long time — which is how a single unwell child becomes a hotel-wide problem.
  • Teach them not to swallow the water, in the pool and in the sea. Obvious, and genuinely effective.
  • Toddler splash pools are the highest-risk water on any resort — warm, shallow, crowded with children in nappies. If something is going round the hotel, that is usually where.

When it is not just a bug — get seen today

  • Signs of dehydration: no wet nappy or no urine for many hours, no tears when crying, dry mouth, sunken eyes, unusual drowsiness.
  • Nothing at all stays down — vomiting that will not stop.
  • Blood in the stool or the vomit, or green vomit.
  • Severe or persistent tummy pain, or a swollen, tender abdomen.
  • High fever alongside the illness, or a rash that does not fade when pressed.
  • Any baby under one year — the margin is much smaller, so the threshold to be seen is lower.

For a seizure, breathing difficulty or a child you cannot wake, call 112 — free, nationwide, day and night.

Hotel doctor, or out to a hospital?

Most resorts either have a doctor on site or will call one for you within the hour. That is genuinely useful, and also limited — knowing which is which saves an evening.

Good forReassurance; a child who is unwell but drinking; a prescriptionA child not keeping fluids down, dehydration, under-1s, anything not improving
SpeedComes to the room, usually within the hourYou travel; çocuk acil is open 24 hours with no appointment
Cannot doAdmit your child, give a drip, run laboratory tests—

Whichever you use, keep the itemised invoice and the written report. Your travel insurer will ask for both, and they are far harder to obtain once you have flown home. Flying home

Usually fine once your child is keeping fluids down and is no longer actively vomiting — but it is worth planning rather than hoping.

  • Airlines can refuse to carry a passenger who is visibly very unwell. A quiet, rehydrated child is a different proposition at the gate.
  • Cabin air is dehydrating. A child who is borderline on the ground will be worse at altitude, so rehydrate properly before departure.
  • In hand luggage: oral rehydration sachets, water, spare clothes, bags, wipes. Not in the hold.
  • If you are unsure, ask a doctor whether they are fit to fly — and if you need to change flights, that opinion in writing is what the insurer will want.
  • Keep every invoice and report from the trip.

What actually prevents it

Four things carry most of the benefit. The first is the one almost everyone gets wrong.

  • Soap and water, not just hand gel. Alcohol gel does not reliably kill norovirus — the virus behind most sudden resort outbreaks of vomiting. Washing physically removes it; gel does not. Use gel as a supplement, never as the substitute, and wash before every meal and after every toilet visit.
  • Hot food hot, fruit you peel. See the buffet section — temperature is most of the story.
  • Bottled water, and mind the ice. Most households in Turkey do not drink the tap water even though it is treated; for babies, and for a child already ill, use sealed bottled water for drinking, for formula and for making up ORS.
  • Pack ORS sachets from home. They weigh nothing, and you will not want to be finding a pharmacy at midnight in a resort town on day one.
